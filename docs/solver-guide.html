KiPI Solver Guide

What power integrity analysis is, why it is worth doing before a board is built, and how KiPI's DC, thermal, AC and Coupled Noise solvers turn a PCB layout into voltages, currents, temperatures, impedances and the noise a switcher couples onto signal nets.

This page explains the ideas. The user guide explains every button, the setup and the results, and the README how to install KiPI.

What power integrity is

Every chip on a board needs its supply voltage held within a tolerance, typically a few percent, whatever current it draws. A voltage regulator (VRM) sets that voltage, but the chip does not sit at the regulator's pins: between them is the power distribution network (PDN), made of traces, planes, vias, the capacitors around the chip and the copper that carries the current back to ground.

That copper is not a perfect conductor. It has resistance, so a steady current drops voltage along the way, and it has inductance, so a current that changes quickly drops voltage too. Power integrity (PI) analysis predicts those drops and checks that what arrives at each chip is still in tolerance:

  • DC: the steady drop from resistance, V = I·R, and the heat the current makes in the copper.
  • AC: the drop from a changing current, set by the PDN's impedance across frequency, ΔV = Z(f)·ΔI.

The whole budget is shared: a 1.0 V rail with ±3% tolerance has 30 mV to spend, split between the regulator's own accuracy, the DC drop and the AC ripple and droop.

Why it matters

Supply voltages have fallen while currents have risen. A processor core at 0.8 V and 40 A has a total tolerance of about 25 mV. At that current, a single milliohm of copper uses 40 mV, more than the whole budget. A rail that is out of tolerance rarely fails cleanly. Instead it causes intermittent resets, bit errors, jitter on clocks and serial links, and failures that come and go with temperature or workload. These faults are hard to find on the bench and expensive to fix with a respin.

The layout decides most of the PDN, through plane shapes, neck-downs, via counts and where the capacitors go. PI simulation looks at it before fabrication, while fixing a problem means moving copper rather than adding bodge wires.

All four use the same model of the board: the copper of each net on each layer, read from KiCad (or a board file), the stackup's thicknesses and materials, and a power tree you describe once. The power tree says which pads are VRM outputs, which are loads and what they draw, and which parts (sense resistors, ferrite beads, load switches) join one net to another.

The triangle mesh

Copper shapes on a real board are irregular: planes with antipads, traces that bend, thermal reliefs, pads of every shape. No formula gives the resistance of such a shape, so KiPI cuts it into many small triangles, works out how current flows across each one, and combines them. This is the finite element method (FEM).

KiPI meshes each net on each copper layer with Jonathan Shewchuk's Triangle, a quality Delaunay mesher:

  • Quality. No triangle has an angle under 28° (Mesh tab, Min angle), since long thin triangles give poor answers. No edge is longer than Max edge (0.5 mm by default), so large planes still get enough triangles to show how current spreads.
  • Grading. Current crowds around vias and pads, so triangles shrink toward every drill hole, down to Min edge (0.02 mm), and grow again away from it. Detail is spent where the voltage changes fastest.
  • Pads and holes. Each load or VRM pad outline is built into the mesh, so the pad has nodes of its own to inject or draw current. Drill holes are holes in the copper, and the nodes around each rim are where the plated barrel joins that layer.
  • 2.5D. Each layer is a flat sheet with the copper's thickness. Vias and plated holes join the sheets of different layers through their barrels. Current flowing through the thickness of a single sheet is not modeled, which suits board copper, which is tens of microns thick and millimeters wide.

The number of nodes (unknowns) sets the solve time, from thousands on a small board to millions on a large one with fine meshing. A coarser Max edge solves faster; a finer one resolves narrow necks and small planes better.

DC: voltage drop

At DC, current in a copper sheet flows from high to low voltage, spreading out wherever the copper is wider. The voltage V on the sheet obeys

∇·(Gs ∇V) = 0, with sheet conductance Gs = t / ρ

where t is the copper thickness and ρ its resistivity (1.72×10⁻⁸ Ω·m for copper at 20 °C). 1 oz copper (35 µm) has a sheet resistance of about 0.5 mΩ per square: any square of it, of any size, measures 0.5 mΩ edge to edge. A trace 0.2 mm wide and 20 mm long is 100 squares, or about 50 mΩ.

On each triangle the voltage is taken to vary linearly. That turns the equation into a small conductance matrix per triangle, like three resistors joining its corners. Adding them up gives one large sparse system G·V = I: the conductance matrix, the unknown node voltages, and the currents injected at the pads. Then:

  • A via or plated hole joins the rim nodes on each layer it passes through, through its barrel's resistance ρ·L / (π(d·tp − tp²)) for drill d and plating tp. A 0.3 mm via through 1.6 mm of board is about 1.3 mΩ.
  • A VRM output holds its pads at its set voltage (a fixed node voltage).
  • A load draws its current from its pads, shared equally between them or split into groups of pads with different current requirements.
  • A series part (a sense resistor, load switch or a ferrite bead's DC resistance) joins the pads on both sides through its resistance, so the nets it joins are solved as one.

Solving gives the voltage at every node. The current density comes from the voltage gradient, the via currents from the barrels, and the power lost in the copper from I²R summed over every triangle and barrel.

The power tree

Boards have more than one rail, and rails feed each other: a 12 V input feeds a buck to 3.3 V, which feeds an LDO to 1.8 V. KiPI solves them downstream first:

  • An LDO draws from its input what it delivers plus its quiescent current.
  • A switching regulator draws Vout·Iout / (η·Vin). Its input voltage depends on the drop on the input rail, which depends on the current it draws, so KiPI iterates the two until they agree.
  • Remote sense moves a VRM's output until its sense pins sit at the set voltage, as the real control loop does. This creates voltage boost in some places, and drop in other places.
  • Ground is a net like any other: loads return their current through their ground pins, so ground drops count too.

Tolerance analysis

Each load can have a tolerance: how far its supply may sit from the nominal voltage, as ± percent or in volts, taken from the load's datasheet. After the solve, KiPI checks every supply pin of the load. The worst pin's deviation from the VRM's set voltage (from DC drop on the rail, rise on ground, and a VRM load line if it has one) must be within the tolerance for the load to PASS. Any pin outside it makes the load FAIL, and the result tables show which pin and by how much.

Tolerance analysis answers "does this layout leave the chip enough margin?" rather than "what is the voltage?". Two points matter when choosing the number:

  • The datasheet tolerance covers everything, including the regulator's set-point accuracy and the AC ripple and droop. The DC drop may only use part of it. If a 1.0 V ±5% rail's regulator is ±1% accurate and you budget 2% for AC, the DC drop gets the remaining 2%: enter 2 for the load, not 5.
  • The regulator tolerance, the ±1% in the above bullet, can be derived from feeedback resistor tolerances and the regulator's datasheet specifications for its reference voltage accuracy.
  • Use the worst-case current, not the typical one, since drop scales with current. With electro-thermal on, the check uses the copper at its solved temperature, which is when its resistance is highest.

VRMs are checked too: whether the input left after the input rail's drop is still above the regulator's dropout and within its input range. A VRM that cannot hold its output fails every load downstream of it.

Electro-thermal co-simulation

The power lost in the copper, I²R, turns into heat. On a high-current rail that heat raises the board temperature by tens of degrees, and the heat from neighboring planes, regulators and loads adds to it. KiPI's thermal model spreads that heat across the whole board, with a temperature for each copper layer l:

∇·(Gl ∇Tl) + Σm (Tm − Tl) / Rlm − (Tl − Tamb) / (1/h + Rcoat) + q″l = 0

  • Gl is how well layer l spreads heat sideways: its copper (weighted by how much of each spot is copper, on any net) plus half the in-plane conductivity of each dielectric it touches, from its material in the stackup.
  • Rlm is the resistance through the laminate to the neighboring copper layers m above and below, from each dielectric's through-plane conductivity. Glass laminates conduct about half as well through their thickness as along it (FR4 core 0.3 W/m·K against 0.8). Every plated hole, via or component hole, on any net, adds its barrel's copper in parallel, which is why thermal vias work.
  • h is the convection to the air, about 10 W/m²K in still air (with radiation) and rising with the airflow you set. Only the top and bottom layers touch the air.
  • Rcoat is the solder mask and silkscreen over an outer layer, in series with the air: t/k for each, a mask at 0.25 W/m·K over the whole face and a silkscreen at 0.2 in the cells where it prints (read from the board, usually a few percent of it). It matters little: a 25 µm mask is 0.1 K·mm²/W, against 100 K·mm²/W (1/h) from the surface into still air.
  • q″l is the heat put into layer l: each layer's copper loss, via losses shared by the layers the via spans, and the heat of series parts, VRMs and, optionally, loads on the layers of their pads.

Spread out, the layers sit within a degree or two of each other: across 1.6 mm of FR4 the resistance is about a tenth of the resistance from the surface into the air. Concentrated, they do not. A regulator on the top layer heats its own pads and the top copper around it; the planes below see that heat only after it crosses the laminate or goes down vias. KiPI reports each layer's own peak.

Hot copper resists more

Copper's resistivity rises with temperature, by about 0.393% per °C near room temperature:

R(T) = R20 · (1 + α (T − 20 °C)), α ≈ 0.00393 /°C

Copper that runs 50 °C hotter than 20 °C has about 20% more resistance, and at 100 °C about 31% more. The physics behind it: as the copper heats, its atoms vibrate harder and scatter the electrons carrying the current more often.

That forms a loop: current heats the copper, the heat raises its resistance, more resistance means more drop and more I²R loss, and that loss heats it further. KiPI's co-simulation follows the loop:

  1. Solve the DC power tree at the current temperature map (20 °C everywhere at first).
  2. Gather every loss on the board onto one grid, on the layer it is made on, and solve each layer's temperature.
  3. Set each triangle's resistivity from its own layer's temperature where it sits, and go back to step 1.

Each pass reports how much the peak temperature changed. On most boards it settles within a few passes, which is when DC drop and temperature agree. A rail that keeps heating from pass to pass is a warning in itself: its copper is too narrow for its current.

The temperature coefficient can be set per layer in the Stackup window, for conductors other than copper.

AC: PDN impedance

What PDN impedance is

Chips draw current in bursts: a processor that wakes up, an FPGA's clock that starts, an amplifier at full swing. The regulator's control loop corrects slowly, typically within tens or hundreds of kHz, so faster changes in current are supplied by the capacitors and the planes. How much the voltage moves for a given change in current depends on frequency:

ΔV(f) = Z(f) · ΔI(f)

PDN impedance Z(f) is the impedance a chip sees looking out of its power pins into the power supply network. It is the ratio of voltage change to current change, and it depends on every capacitor, plane and via between them and ground. It is a complex number at each frequency: KiPI shows its magnitude |Z| and its resistive and reactive parts.

A real capacitor is not a pure capacitance. It also has an equivalent series inductance (ESL, from its construction, termination, and mounting) and resistance (ESR). These factors vary greatly from capaitor to capacitor and case size to case size. Below its self-resonant frequency it acts as a capacitor and its impedance falls with frequency. Above it, it acts as an inductor and its impedance rises. That is why a PDN uses many values: bulk capacitors for low frequencies, many small ceramics for the middle, and the plane pair's own capacitance for the top. Where one hands over to the next, the curve has peaks (anti-resonances), and those peaks are where a rail is most likely to fail.

Target impedance

If the voltage may move by ΔV while the load steps by ΔI, the PDN must stay below the target impedance

Ztarget = ΔV / ΔI

up to the highest frequency in the step. A 1.0 V rail allowed 3% (30 mV) of transient for a 10 A step needs Ztarget = 3 mΩ. A step that rises in time tr has most of its energy below f ≈ 1 / (π·tr), which is how far the target applies. KiPI's target calculator works both out from the load's transient spec (after Smith et al., 1999). Some chip vendors publish a frequency-dependent target mask instead, which KiPI takes as corner points.

KiPI leaves the regulator out of the AC model and computes the open-loop PDN impedance. Below the regulator's control-loop bandwidth, the loop is assumed to hold the voltage. Above it, the loop is too slow, which is the range a target applies to, so you give each VRM's bandwidth and the targets are judged from there up. Other analysis methods can model the interaction with the control loop and the capacitive portion of hte PDN, this is called closed-loop analysis and is outside the scope of KiPI.

How KiPI models it

The AC solver uses the same triangle mesh, extended for the fields between rail and ground copper:

  • Two faces per sheet. At high frequency, current crowds onto the copper surfaces (the skin effect: about 6.6 µm deep at 100 MHz) and onto the face looking at the return current. Each copper sheet therefore has a top and a bottom face, joined at its edges, holes and pads, and the copper's internal impedance moves from its DC resistance to surface resistance with frequency.
  • Inductance. A face looking at ground copper a distance h away has a loop inductance of μ₀h per square, 126 pH per square for 0.1 mm of dielectric. Thin dielectrics between power and ground keep the inductance low.
  • Capacitance. Facing rail and ground copper is a capacitor of ε₀εr·area / h, with the dielectric's loss tangent. 100 cm² of 0.1 mm FR4 is about 3.8 nF, a small capacitor of very low inductance that matters at hundreds of MHz.
  • Vias join the faces of each layer through their barrels. Their inductance, and the inductance of a capacitor's mounting, comes out of how current spreads to them on the planes. You enter only each capacitor's own self ESL, not its mounting.
  • Capacitors are series RLC models at their pads. A ferrite bead or resistor between nets is a series link and can include DCR and ESL.

For two facing planes this is the classic plane-pair (cavity) model, so plane resonances come out of the mesh, as does the spreading inductance between a capacitor and the chip. The sweep factorizes the copper model at a few frequencies per decade, solves for every capacitor and probe port at once, interpolates that smoothly, and adds the capacitors at each of the many frequencies plotted. This keeps a sweep from 1 kHz to 300 MHz at a few hundred points per decade fast. Note this this creates a massive computational problem. AC solves can take a long time.

Effective vs nominal capacitance

The capacitance printed on a ceramic capacitor (MLCC) is measured at 1 kHz, 25 °C, with no DC voltage, when the part is new. On a board it is often much less. KiPI uses the capacitance you enter for each model, so enter the effective value at your rail's conditions. Four effects stack up:

The effects multiply. A worst case for that 10 µF X5R part on a 3.3 V rail at 85 °C:

10 µF × 0.45 (DC bias) × 0.85 (temperature) × 0.92 (aging) × 0.80 (tolerance) ≈ 2.8 µF

More than two thirds of the capacitance is gone. Lower capacitance raises the impedance below the part's resonance and moves the resonance up, by the square root of the ratio, while the inductance above resonance does not change. Capacitor makers publish DC-bias and temperature curves per part, and their online tools (Murata SimSurfing, TDK SEAT, KEMET K-SIM, Samsung's tool) give the capacitance, ESR and ESL at a chosen bias and temperature. Enter the manufacturer's delectric, tolerance, aging and DC bias factors. KiPI can work out how each impacts the PDN impedance.

Coupled Noise: a switcher as aggressor

A buck or boost converter switches amps in nanoseconds. Its switch node swings the full input (buck) or output (boost) voltage on every edge, and its hot loop carries a pulsed current. Both reach nearby signal nets: the switch node's electric field through mutual capacitance (a current Cm·dV/dt), its loop's magnetic field through mutual inductance (a voltage M·di/dt), and the shared ground through common impedance. The victim is far weaker than the aggressor, so the problem is solved one way (weak coupling): the converter's waveforms are built first, and each coupling path then acts on the victim as a source, harmonic by harmonic. The noise from each path is kept apart, so the dominant one shows.

How a solve runs

One converter, the aggressor, is solved at a time, against every victim set up for it (and, with the scan on, the worst of the nets near it). A solve takes these steps, and the log reports each:

  1. Operating point. The input and output voltages come from the VRM (or your override) and decide buck or boost; the output current is what the DC loads on the output rail draw, unless you set it.
  2. Hot loop. Each phase's loop through its input (buck) or output (boost) capacitors is traced on the copper, giving its inductance, resistance and the switch node's capacitance, and so the frequency and damping of the ringing on every edge.
  3. Waveforms. The switch-node voltage, inductor current, pulsed current and each capacitor's ripple current are built as spectra of harmonics of the switching frequency, ringing included. They are shown at once, before any coupling is solved.
  4. Board model (optional). AC mode's field model of the rails and ground shares the ripple current among the capacitors through the real copper, and gives the ground voltage between each victim's ends.
  5. Scan (optional). Every signal net near the converter is estimated cheaply and the worst are added as victims of the scan's type, undriven by default.
  6. Victims. For each one, the coupling to its traces is extracted (mutual capacitance by 2D field cuts, mutual inductance by PEEC), its line is solved at every harmonic once per mechanism, and the result is turned back into a waveform at the receiver and checked against its noise margin.

Because the coupling is weak, each step only feeds the next: the victims never load the converter, and each mechanism can be solved and judged alone. The sections below follow the same order.

The aggressor's waveforms

From the input and output voltages, the duty cycle is D = Vout/Vin for a buck (raised by its losses) and 1 − η·Vin/Vout for a boost. Each phase's switch node is a trapezoid with the entered rise and fall times, its inductor current a triangle of ripple ΔI = (Vin − Vout)·D/(L·fsw) (buck) or Vin·D/(L·fsw) (boost), and its pulsed current the inductor current while the hot-loop switch conducts (plus the ringing's current, below). These are piecewise linear, so their Fourier series is exact in closed form: the second derivative is a set of impulses at the corners, and ck = −1/(T·ωk²)·Σ Δsi·e−jωkti. N phases are interleaved by delaying phase n by n·T/N, which cancels every harmonic but each N-th in their sum. The harmonics run up to ten times the faster edge's corner frequency, 1/(π·tr), where the trapezoid's spectrum has fallen 40 dB/decade.

The ripple current into each rail's capacitors is shared by their admittances, each capacitor's model plus its loop's inductance and resistance; the rail's ripple voltage is that current through the bank's impedance.

Hot loop and ringing

Each capacitor's loop through the switching device is drawn between their pads, as wide as the net's copper along each straight piece, and its inductance is the sum of the pieces' partial inductances (Ruehli's PEEC): Neumann's double integral M = μ₀/4π·∬ dl·dl′/R, with the inner integral in closed form and each bar's cross-section entering as its geometric mean radius 0.2235·(w + t). A solid plane under the loop is a perfect conductor by images: the mirrored loop, carrying the opposite current, takes away most of the inductance of a flat loop close over it. Against closed forms this gives a bar's self inductance within 0.5 % and a square loop's within 2 %.

The loop's inductance L with the switch node's capacitance C (the device's Coss plus its copper over the neighbouring planes) rings at f = 1/(2π√(L·C)) on every edge, damped by ζ = R/2·√(C/L). The loop's resistance alone gives far less damping than real switch nodes show, so a least damping (ζ = 0.05 by default) stands in for the losses the model leaves out. Each edge passes through H(jω) = ω₀²/(s² + 2ζω₀s + ω₀²), the switch node's response through the loop's L–C tank.

The two edges are not alike (TI, SLPA010). The control switch's turn-on (a buck's high side, a boost's low side) is hard while the valley current flows into the inductor: the other switch's body diode is conducting it, and the control switch must take that current over and sweep out the diode's reverse-recovery charge Qrr before the node can move. The diode keeps conducting in reverse until the charge is gone, the current ramping at the edge's di/dt = I/t, then snaps off over the edge, so Qrr = ½·Irr·(Irr/(di/dt) + t) gives the recovery current's peak Irr. The hot loop carries this triangle of current. The node, held by the conducting diode until the snap, answers the snap alone: a current Irr leaving the diode for the node's capacitance, through the tank's impedance jωL·H. That rings the hard edge about Irr·Z0 higher. The edge's own damping is the least ringing damping.

The control switch's turn-off is soft while the peak current flows into the inductor. The inductor current itself carries the node across, so the edge is no faster than C·V/I, into the other switch's body diode, which turns on and clamps it. The clamp takes the ringing's energy: the soft edge is damped at least the soft-edge damping (ζ = 0.5 by default, one overshoot and settled). A valley current below zero (forced PWM at light load) makes the turn-on soft too: zero-voltage switching. But the controller waits only its dead time (20 ns by default) before turning the next switch on. A current too small to carry the node across in that time (C·V/I longer than the dead time) leaves the switch to finish the edge: it is hard, at the entered edge time and the least ringing damping, with no recovery, since no diode was conducting. The trapezoid's spectrum is a sum of its corners' terms, so each edge's two corners pass through their own H.

The ringing is the switch node's capacitance trading charge with the loop's inductance, so its current flows round the hot loop, through the capacitors that close it. By KCL at the switch node, with the inductor current steady over an edge, a buck's high side carries C·dv/dt more and a boost's rectifier C·dv/dt less, where v is the ringing, the switch node's departure from its ideal trapezoid: Iring = jω·C·(H − 1)·Videal at each harmonic. Its peak is about V/Z0, with Z0 = √(L/C): a few amps for a 12 V switch node ringing at a few hundred MHz. It is part of the pulsed current, so the input capacitors of a buck (the output capacitors of a boost) carry it, and with it the inductive coupling and the board model.

Capacitive coupling

A victim's traces are cut into short segments. Across the middle of each, a line through the board finds the copper on every layer within a window: the switch nodes, the planes and power and ground copper (AC ground), and the victim. That cut is a 2D electrostatic problem: ∇·(ε∇φ) = 0 in the stackup's dielectrics (the solder mask over the outer layers, air beyond), solved with triangles graded fine at the conductors' corners, where the field is singular. Holding the victim at 1 V and everything else at 0 gives its capacitance C′ and its mutual capacitance Cm′ to each switch node per mm, from the charge on each; the same cut in vacuum gives C0′ and so its inductance L′ = μ₀ε₀/C0′. Cuts that see the same copper are solved once. Against closed forms the cut gives microstrip and stripline impedance within 2 % (Hammerstad–Jensen, Wheeler) and two wires' mutual capacitance within 1 %.

Inductive coupling

Each input and output capacitor closes a current loop: in a buck the input capacitors' loops through the switching device carry the pulsed input current, with edges as fast as the switch's, and the output capacitors' loops through the inductor carry its ripple. Each loop carries its capacitor's share of the current. A ground return with no ground copper under it on the loop's layer is taken through the plane, under the loop, as the return current flows at high frequency.

A victim segment and its return current, in the plane under it, link the loops' flux. The partial mutual inductance from each loop segment to each victim segment is Neumann's integral, as for the hot loop. The plane answers the loops' field with eddy currents, an image of the loop: a thin copper sheet is transparent to a loop ℓ across at low frequency and a mirror at high, reflecting Γ = jα/(1 + jα) of it with α = ω·μ₀·(t/ρ)·ℓ/2 (about 0.3 at 10 MHz for a 4 mm loop over 35 µm of copper). So a victim on the loops' side picks up −jω·I·(M − Γ·Mimage) along each segment, and one on the far side of the plane (1 − Γ)/cosh((1+j)·t/δ) of what it would link there: very little once the plane mirrors the field and the skin effect stops what is left. The EMF is a series source in the victim's line: an open receiver sees nearly all of it, as V = E·ZR/(ZD + ZR).

Checked against closed forms: two lines over a plane (μ₀/4π·ln(1 + 4h²/s²) per length) within 5 %, far loops as dipoles (−μ₀A₁A₂/4πd³) within 5 %, and a strip's inductance over a plane within 10 % of μ₀ε₀/C₀′ from the field solve (PEEC spreads the current across the strip, the field solve puts it on the surface).

The board model and shared-ground coupling

The converter's input and output rails and their ground are AC mode's 2.5D field model (above), with ports: per phase, the pulsed current between the switching device's high-side and ground pins and the inductor current between the inductor's rail pad and the device's ground; every capacitor on the rails; and, for each victim, its driver's ground pads against its receiver's. The board's port impedance matrix Z is solved at a few frequencies per decade and interpolated onto the harmonics. With the capacitors on their ports (impedance Ze), a source current Is divides among them as IE = −(ZEE + Ze)⁻¹ ZEs Is, through the copper's real resistance and inductance, and the voltage at an observing port is the terminated transfer impedance Zos − ZoE(ZEE + Ze)⁻¹ZEs times the source currents. That gives each capacitor's ripple current (which the inductive coupling then uses), the rails' ripple at the device, and ΔV between a victim's grounds. The AC probes ticked for an aggressor are observing ports too, open between the probe's rail and ground pins. Their terminated transfer impedance times the source currents gives the ripple at the load, through the plane's spreading inductance and the capacitors nearer the load.

Only the transfer impedance's resistive part is taken between a victim's grounds. The inductive part of a ground's potential depends on the path it is measured along: the victim's own loop, its trace over the plane, links that flux, which the inductive coupling already counts. (The 2.5D model would also overstate it where a ground plane faces no rail copper: its current spreads wider than a real return, which stays under its forward path.) What is left is the copper's IR drop, skin effect included, between the two grounds.

The receiver judges the signal against its own ground; the driver drives it from its own. So a ground difference ΔV drives the victim's line through the driver's output resistance, a current ΔV/ZD at the driver: a high-impedance receiver sees nearly all of ΔV. With a single capacitor on each rail the board model's split matches the lumped one at the low harmonics, as it must: there is nowhere else for the current to go.

Scanning for victims

A scan estimates every signal net with a trace near the aggressor's parts, then solves the worst in full. The estimate's capacitive part uses Maxwell's potential coefficients for two wires a distance d apart at height h over a plane, Cm′ = 2πε·p₁₂/(p₁₁² − p₁₂²) with p₁₁ = ln(2h/a), p₁₂ = ½·ln(1 + 4h²/d²) and a = w/4, from each trace segment to the nearest switch-node copper. The edge drives Cm·dV/dt into the net through the scan's victim type's resistance R (its driver and receiver in parallel), which also charges the net's own capacitance Cg (each segment's 2πε/p₁₁ per length, plus the receiver's): over an edge of t = ΔV/(dV/dt), v = Cm·R·dV/dt·(1 − e−t/τ) with τ = R·(Cm + Cg). A low R gives Cm·R·dV/dt; an undriven net the charge divider ΔV·Cm/(Cm + Cg). Its inductive part is the full inductive model, which is cheap. The estimate only ranks the nets (its capacitive part errs high, on the safe side); the results are the full solves.

A scanned net's real driver is not known, so by default it is solved as undriven (high impedance: 1 MΩ at each end and 5 pF), the worst case for capacitive coupling: nothing holds the line, so the coupled charge divides between the coupling and the line's own capacitance. It is not the worst case for inductive coupling. An EMF along a floating line divides by where the line's capacitance sits, while a low-impedance driver at the far end puts all of it at the receiver. A net that is a victim already keeps its own type, and the scan's type can be changed in Settings.

The victim's line

Each segment is a lossy transmission line, Z0 = √((R′ + jωL′)/(G′ + jωC′)) and γ = √((R′ + jωL′)(G′ + jωC′)), joined where their ends meet into the victim's whole net. The driver is its output impedance to ground; the receiver its input resistance and capacitance, with any termination. The switch node's charge enters each segment as a current jω·Cm′·l·Vsw, a loop's flux as an EMF in series with each segment, a ground difference through the driver, and the network is solved at every harmonic, once for each mechanism. For a short line this is the lumped weak-coupling result, V = jω·Cm·(ZD ∥ ZR)·Vsw (Paul): a strong driver holds the line down, and an open-drain line released high, held only by its pull-up, picks up far more. The receiver's noise over a period is compared with its noise margin, from its logic family's JESD8 levels: min(VIL − VOL, VOH − VIH).

The line is also solved as it really sits: held high at VOH, with VIH below it, and held low at VOL, with VIL above it. Only noise toward the threshold counts in each state, so each state's excursion is the noise's largest step that way, against that state's own margin. A driver may hold the line harder one way than the other: an open drain is held high by its pull-up and low by its switch. The line is then solved once per driver resistance. The verdict takes the larger noise of either state against the smaller margin.

From harmonics to a verdict

Every source is periodic at the switching frequency, so the victim's response is too: at each harmonic k·fsw the network gives a complex voltage at the receiver, one per mechanism. The waveform over one period is their inverse Fourier series, sampled at least four times per highest harmonic:

v(t) = Σk 2·Re(Vk·ejωkt)

The phases matter. Capacitive noise follows the switch node's dV/dt and inductive noise the loop's di/dt, so they peak at different moments of an edge and can add or partly cancel. The total is therefore the sum of the mechanisms' complex spectra, turned into one waveform, and its peak is not the sum of the mechanisms' own peaks. Each mechanism's peak is still reported alone, and the largest names the dominant mechanism, the one to fix first.

The victim's verdict is its total peak |v| at the receiver over a period against its noise margin: PASS at or below 100 %. The peak is usually a short burst on each switching edge, the edge's own coupling followed by the ringing, with quiet between. A receiver that samples away from the edges may tolerate more, and a clock or an edge-triggered input less, so the margin is yours to set when the logic levels' default does not fit.

The Victim noise plot shows the receiver's waveform per mechanism and in total, with the margin; the Spectrum option shows the same as harmonics, where a peak at the ringing frequency or a line resonance points at its cause. Victims the scan added are marked (scan) in the results, and the report's Scan table ranks every net it estimated, with the ones solved in full marked.

The 3D view shows where along the victim the noise couples in. Its line network is linear and reciprocal: the voltage a unit source at segment i puts at the receiver equals the voltage a unit current injected at the receiver puts at segment i. So one extra solve per harmonic, driven from the receiver, gives the transfer from every segment at once. Each segment's sources (its charge current, its EMF, the driver's ground difference) times that transfer give its contribution to the receiver's spectrum; taken at the instant of the peak, the contributions add up exactly to the peak. A segment that adds to the peak is red, one that takes from it blue.

Linear solvers and PARDISO

Every analysis ends in a large sparse system of equations, one unknown per mesh node. DC and thermal systems are symmetric positive definite; AC systems are complex. How KiPI solves them:

SolverHow it worksUsed for
SuperLU (SciPy)A sparse direct solver: it factorizes the matrix once, then each right-hand side is a fast forward and back substitution. Single-threaded. Always installed.Systems up to about 200k unknowns without PARDISO.
AMG-CGConjugate gradients, an iterative solver, preconditioned by algebraic multigrid (pyamg). Uses much less memory than a factorization, but has to iterate for every right-hand side.DC and thermal systems above the direct limit.
PARDISO (Intel MKL)A parallel sparse direct solver. It factorizes on every CPU core and handles complex systems too.Up to 1M unknowns, if installed, and larger DC and thermal systems while the factorization fits in half the free memory.

PARDISO is an optional install (the pypardiso package). Its benefits:

  • Speed on large boards. Factorizing is the slow step, and PARDISO uses every core. On a 645k-unknown rail one solve drops from about 8 s to 1.2 s, and a four-pass electro-thermal run from about 90 s to 34 s.
  • A higher direct-solve limit. With PARDISO, KiPI factorizes systems of up to 1M unknowns instead of 200k, and larger DC and thermal systems while the factorization, reckoned at 2 kB an unknown, fits in half the memory free. On KiCad's Jetson AGX Thor demo a 4.9M-unknown ground factorizes in 10 s where AMG-CG took 104 s. If such a factorization fails, that system is solved by AMG-CG, not by SuperLU. A factorization is reused for every load, power-tree iteration and AC port, which an iterative solver cannot do, so fine meshes stay fast.
  • AC sweeps. Each field frequency of an AC sweep is a complex factorization, so large AC models gain the most.

The drawbacks: Intel MKL is about 600 MB installed, it exists only for x86-64 Windows and Linux (not macOS or ARM), and small boards, which solve in a second either way, gain little. KiPI offers it, ticked, the first time it starts (or sets up, standalone) on a computer that can have it, and it can be turned down there or removed later under About → Manage dependencies…. KiPI falls back to SuperLU on its own if PARDISO is missing, cannot load, or returns an inaccurate result, and the results are the same either way. Each factorization is checked by its backward error, the smallest relative change to the system that would make the solution exact; a working solve gives about 1e-16 however stiff the board's copper and vias make the system. Because PARDISO utilizes all cores, the machine running the simulation will really stretch its legs. Expect high CPU utilization and higher junction temperatures, at the gain of simulation speed. About → Copy version info says which solver is in use.

What KiPI does not model

  • DC: copper is solid and its thickness uniform. Plating variation and etch undercut are not modeled; KiPI uses the stackup's thicknesses.
  • Thermal: steady state only, with one temperature through the board's thickness and no heat carried away by the parts, enclosure or conduction into the mountin. No heatsinks; this tool is meant to model copper losses and expose weak points, not simulate the thermal scenario of an entire system.
  • AC: the regulator is left out (open-loop impedance). The package and die are not in the model; probes sit on the board's pads. Capacitors are fixed RLC models, so DC bias and aging are for you to apply to the value you enter. Radiation and coupling to signal nets are not modeled.
  • Coupled Noise: the converter is ideal switches with set edges, not a transistor-level model, and its hot loop is drawn straight between pads. Coupling is quasi-static (no radiation; long lines are transmission lines, but each cut is 2D). The capacitor loops' current is their capacitor's; the inductor's own field (a shielded inductor's leakage) is not modeled. Shared-ground coupling is through the converter's ground net only, between the victim's two ends.

Like any simulation, the results are only as good as the setup. Check that the load currents are worst case, the capacitor values effective, and the stackup right. The tool is incredibly powerful with thoughtful and complete inputs.
